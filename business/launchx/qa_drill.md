# The eight hardest questions

*For rehearsal on 23 October 2026. Drafted 2026-10-12.*

How to use this: cover the answers. Read a question out loud, start a 30-second timer, answer
without notes. Do all eight, twice. The words below are a starting point. Change them until they
sound like you, because an answer in your own words is easier to remember.

The session is 15 minutes and is meant for feedback, so these will come as honest questions,
not attacks. The answers are the same either way. Keep them short so there is time left to hear
what the panel thinks.

One rule for all of them: **"I don't know yet, and here is how I find out"** is a strong answer.
A confident guess that falls apart on the next question is a weak one.

A blank like `[____]` is filled in from the survey or from your cost figures.

---

### 1. "Why wouldn't I just use ChatGPT or Gemini?"

> "For some students you should. If you already know your three universities, a chatbot is
> enough, and I tell people that. Gemini answers what you ask. Most students do not know what to
> ask: whether the degree is recognised back home, what the whole degree costs in ringgit, which
> scholarships they qualify for. I give them a file with those answers, one they can show their
> parents."

Do not attack the chatbot. Agreeing first makes the rest land.

### 2. "All your users are friends and nobody has paid. How do you know anyone wants this?"

> "I don't know yet, and I want to be straight about that. What I know is that the product is
> usable: 29 signed up, the ratings average 4.1 and 4.2 out of 5, and one student came back for a
> second report without being asked. To test real demand I surveyed `[__]` people, `[__]` of them
> strangers, and `[__]` left a number to buy at RM50. The pilot test in November is where they
> actually pay or don't."

This is the question most likely to come up. Say the weakness before they do.

### 3. "It takes you hours per student, and you are one person. How does this grow?"

> "Each student takes me under an hour of hands-on work, and the tools cost about RM158 a month
> in total. What slows me down is not the work. It is that each student needs me at my laptop at
> three or four separate moments. So growing means cutting those moments: one sitting a day
> now, then automating the checking and sending. I want to prove people will pay first, and
> automate second."

### 4. "RM50 times your market is tiny. Where is the business?"

> "You are right, at RM50 it is small: about RM775,000 a year for every Malaysian who starts a
> degree abroad. So the lever is price. Families who pay for help today pay RM2,000 or more. The
> parent carries a RM200,000 to RM400,000 risk, and I have only ever priced for the student.
> What a parent would pay is what I am testing now. `[__]` of `[__]` parents said yes at RM50."

Agreeing with the number, then showing you already did the sums, is the strongest move here.

### 5. "Education agents do this for free. Why would anyone pay you?"

> "Agents are free because the universities pay them. Their own websites say so. That is a fair
> business and they do real work, including filing the application, which I don't. But their
> list starts from their partner universities. Mine starts from the student. I am the only
> option in that table paid by the family."

### 6. "Who is your customer: the student or the parent?"

> "I started with the student. What I have learned is that the student uses it, but the parent
> feels the risk and holds the money, the same way tuition centres work. `[What the parent chats
> showed, in one sentence.]` So I now think the student is the user and the parent is the buyer,
> and the pilot tests that."

If the parent chats have not happened, say so: "That is my current guess, and I test it in the
pilot."

### 7. "What happens when a fee or a deadline you gave turns out to be wrong?"

> "It will happen, because fees change. So every row is labelled: checked on the university's
> own page, or not yet checked, and the student can see which. Before I take money I add a clear
> line saying this is research, not a guarantee, and that fees should be confirmed before
> paying. I would rather show an honest 'not checked' than a confident wrong number."

Do **not** say "every fact is verified". On several lists most rows are still marked not
checked, and one look at a spreadsheet would show it.

### 8. "What would make you stop, or change direction?"

> "If strangers will not pay at any price I try in the pilot, I stop selling the list as it is.
> The thing I would keep is what students told me they worry about most: `[top worry from the
> survey]`. If parents pay and students don't, I rebuild the offer around the parent. I would
> rather find that out in November than in a year."

This is the "kill, persevere or pivot" question the bootcamp is named after. Having an answer
ready shows you are testing an idea, not defending one.

---

## Three more, if there is time

- **"Why you?"** One sentence, true, about why you built it.
- **"What do you want from us?"** Your slide 10 answer, word for word.
- **"What have you built so far?"** "A working product, used by 29 students, and a clickable
  demo." Then stop talking.
