# The Decision Stage: turning research into a plan

*Written 2026-10-10 for a reader with no technical background. Every special word is explained
where it first appears and again in the word list at the end. Figures on current progress come from
the project's own progress report and a count of the student files, both run the same day.*

---

## The short version

By the time a student reaches this stage, we have already done a great deal of research for them.
They have a spreadsheet of universities and a long written report on each of their favourites.

They still do not have an answer.

Three things are missing, and none of them is "more information":

1. **An order.** Which of my favourites should I want most, and why?
2. **A plan.** Which ones do I actually apply to, given that the application systems have rules
   that stop me applying to all of them?
3. **A timetable.** What is the very next thing I have to do, and by what date?

The decision stage produces two short documents that answer those three questions:

- a **recommendation**, which ranks the student's favourites, explains each ranking, and sets out
  how to apply to them;
- a **calendar**, which is a single list of every date that matters, sorted from soonest to latest.

A useful picture: the university reports are guidebooks to five cities. This stage is the
**itinerary**. It says which cities to visit, in what order, and which tickets have to be bought by
which day. Nobody misses a flight because their guidebook was too thin. They miss it because nobody
wrote the departure time down in one place.

One thing this stage never does is choose for the student. It recommends and it explains the
trade-offs. The student and their family decide.

---

## Where this stage sits

The whole service has four steps. Inside the project they are numbered 1, 3, 4 and 5. There is no
step 2, because two early steps were merged into one and the later ones kept their old numbers.

| Step | What happens | What the student gets |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Intake | The student fills in a form about themselves | Nothing yet. We now know who they are |
| 3. Longlist | We find and research universities, one country at a time | The spreadsheet |
| 4. University report | The student picks a few favourites and we research each in depth | A long written report per university |
| **5. Decide and apply (this report)** | **We recommend, and lay out the deadlines** | **A recommendation and a calendar** |

Each step works on fewer universities than the one before. The spreadsheet may hold thirty or
forty. The reports cover a handful. This last step ends with the student holding a short, ordered
list and a set of dates.

Two words from the earlier steps are needed to follow this one:

- A **finalist** is a university the student picked as a favourite, whose facts have been checked
  on the university's own website, and which has a finished report. Strictly, a finalist is one
  course at one university, so two courses at the same university count as two finalists.
- Every university carries a label for how likely this student is to be accepted. The three labels
  are borrowed from admissions advisers: **Reach** (a long shot), **Match** (a realistic chance)
  and **Safety** (very likely).

## Why a stack of reports is not a decision

Imagine the student's position. They have five reports. Each has fifteen sections. Each is honest
about the good and the bad. They have read all of them, and they are more confused than before,
because every university now looks both attractive and risky.

Three specific problems are left.

**The reports never talk to each other.** Each report is about one university. None of them says
"this one is better for you than that one". Comparing is left to the student, at exactly the moment
they have the most to hold in their head.

**The application systems have rules that force choices.** A student cannot simply apply
everywhere. In the United Kingdom they get five choices in total. In the United States every extra
application costs money and requires extra writing. These rules are explained in full below. The
point here is that "which universities do I like?" and "which universities can I apply to?" are
different questions, and only the second one produces an application.

**The dates are scattered.** One university's deadline is in a report. Its scholarship deadline is
on a different page of the same report. The entrance test it requires has its own booking deadline,
set by a different organisation. Multiply that by five universities in three countries. A student
who misses one date they did not know existed has wasted the whole exercise.

This stage exists to solve those three problems, in that order.

## What the student receives

### 1. The recommendation

A written document, usually a few pages. It has five parts.

| Part | What it says | Why it is there |
|---|---|---|
| **The ranked list** | The finalists in recommended order, each with two or three sentences of reasoning | An order is the thing the student could not produce alone |
| **The balance check** | How many of the finalists are Reach, Match and Safety, and whether that spread is safe | Five long shots is five ways to end up with no university at all |
| **The application plan** | Which universities to apply to through which system, within that system's rules | Turns "I like these" into "I am applying to these" |
| **Open risks** | What is still unknown and could change the plan: exam results not yet in, an English test not yet taken, a scholarship not yet decided | So nothing unresolved is mistaken for settled |
| **How to apply, per university** | For each one: the website to apply on, the steps, and the documents needed | A quick reference the student can act on |

The most important rule about the ranked list is that **the reasoning must be tied to what this
student said matters to them.** On the intake form each student ranked what they care about: cost,
job prospects, whether the degree is accepted in Malaysia, the university's ranking, the location,
and so on. A recommendation that says "this is a famous university" is useless. One that says "you
ranked cost first, and this is the only finalist where a scholarship realistically covers the whole
degree" is advice.

### 2. The calendar

One table. Each line has four things: a date, what happens on that date, which university it
belongs to, and what the student has to do.

It holds far more than application deadlines. It also holds scholarship deadlines, the dates when
entrance tests can be booked and sat, interview periods, the earliest date a visa can be applied
for, and the day the course starts. (A **visa** is a government's official permission to enter and
stay in its country, here as a student.)

Underneath the table sits a second, shorter list headed "to confirm". It holds anything that has no
firm date yet, such as a university that has not published next year's deadline. More on why that
list matters below.

## How it gets made

There are four steps. As in the rest of the service, the work is split between two very different
kinds of worker:

- **The AI** does the reading, weighing and writing. It is good at taking five long reports and one
  student's priorities and explaining which fits best.
- **Ordinary programs, with no AI in them,** do the collecting and sorting. A program gives the same
  answer every time and does not get creative with a date.

The founder starts the stage, steers it, and talks the student through the result.

Before anything begins there is one entry check, and the instruction is firm. **If a student has no
finalists with finished reports, stop and go back.** A recommendation built on unchecked facts would
be worse than no recommendation, so the reports get finished first.

### Step 1: One last side-by-side

A program reads the student's spreadsheet, pulls out only the finalists, and lays them next to each
other in one table. Each university is a column. Each row is one fact. The table shows 26 of the
spreadsheet's 35 facts, in eight groups: the summary, cost, scholarships, quality, grades and entry,
daily life, visa and work rights, and deadlines.

No AI is involved in this step and nothing is looked up. It is the same checked information the
student already has, rearranged so that five universities can be read across one line. The founder
walks the student through it. This is the last chance to drop a university before the recommendation
is written.

### Step 2: Writing the recommendation

The AI writes the five-part document described above. It works from three things: the side-by-side
table, the finished reports, and the student's own ranking of what matters to them.

Three rules shape what it writes.

**Say so if the spread is unsafe.** The AI must count the Reach, Match and Safety finalists and
state plainly whether the set is balanced. It is not allowed to rank a lopsided list and stay
quiet about the lopsidedness.

**Make the plan conditional when grades are not final.** Many students apply before they have
their real exam results. They apply on **predicted grades**, meaning the grades they or their
school expect them to get. A plan that only works if every prediction comes true is fragile. So the
plan has to answer a second question: which choices still stand if the results come in lower, and
what is the backup route? The usual backup is a **foundation year**, an extra preparatory year
that some universities offer to students who narrowly miss the entry grades. The previous stage
researches that route whenever a student's grades fall short of what a university asks, so this
stage can point to it.

**Put scholarship dates ahead of payments.** After a university accepts a student, it normally
asks for a **deposit**: money paid up front to hold the place, often not returned if the student
later withdraws. For a student who can only attend with a scholarship, paying a deposit before
knowing the scholarship result is a gamble with the family's money. The plan must order things so
that the scholarship answers come first.

### Step 3: Building the calendar

A program builds the calendar. No AI is involved.

It collects dates from two places:

1. **The spreadsheet.** Every finalist has a "key deadline" on its row. That gives one line per
   university: the application deadline, and the system it must be submitted through.
2. **Notes left behind by the report researchers.** When a researcher wrote a university's report
   in the previous stage, it also recorded every dated event it came across (a scholarship
   deadline, a test booking window, the earliest visa date) in a small structured list. The
   calendar program picks those lists up.

It then sorts everything from earliest to latest and writes the table.

Two design choices are worth knowing about.

**Nothing is thrown away for being vague.** Some dates cannot be placed on a timeline, because the
university wrote "late January" or "not published yet". The program does not guess a date and does
not drop the line. It moves it to the "to confirm" list at the bottom, with the university's
original wording shown. A reader can see at a glance which dates are firm and which still need
chasing.

**It only reads; it never changes anything.** The calendar program cannot alter the spreadsheet or
any report. It can be run again at any time without risk to the research underneath.

It also only looks at the finalists. An earlier version swept up the dates of every university
that had ever been researched for that student, including ones the student had rejected, so a
rejected university's deadline could appear on the calendar. That was found and fixed.

### Step 4: The how-to-apply recap, and the hand-over

The recommendation ends with a short table: for each recommended university, where to apply and
which documents to gather. Typical documents are:

- a **transcript** (the official record of the student's grades);
- a **personal statement** or essays (the student's own writing about why they want the course);
- **references** (letters from teachers);
- an **English test** result, for universities that require proof of English;
- an entrance test, a **portfolio** (a collection of the student's work) or an interview, where
  the course asks for one.

The founder then reviews the calendar with the student and points out the earliest actions. The
stage is finished when both documents exist, the student understands the ranked options and the
plan, and the first deadlines have been clearly flagged.

## The rules of applying, and why they force a strategy

This is the part most families do not know, and it is where a recommendation earns its place.

### The United Kingdom: five choices on one form

Almost every UK university is applied to through one shared service called **UCAS** (the
Universities and Colleges Admissions Service). The student fills in one form and sends it to the
universities they choose.

Two rules matter:

- **A maximum of five choices.** A student with seven UK favourites has to cut two before applying.
- **Oxford or Cambridge, never both.** A student may not apply to both of these two universities
  in the same year.

So a student whose finalists include both Oxford and Cambridge does not have two options there.
They have one slot and a choice to make. The recommendation has to help make it, using the
student's own priorities.

Those two universities also have a much earlier deadline than the rest: mid-October, against
mid-January for most other UK courses. A student who learns that in November has lost a year.

### The United States: every extra application has a price

Most US universities accept a shared form called the **Common App** (short for Common
Application). There is no five-choice limit. The limit is cost and effort:

- each university charges its own **application fee**, a payment simply for being considered;
- many ask for their own extra essays on top of the shared form;
- some require tests.

Ten US applications can mean ten fees and a great deal of extra writing during the student's
final school year. The recommendation is told to count the total cost of applying, and to let that
shape how many applications are sensible. A few US universities do not use the shared form at all
and have their own.

### Singapore, Malaysia and China: one website each

These are **direct applications**: the student applies on each university's own website (often
called a **portal**), and every university sets its own dates. There is no shared form to do the
work once. In China a government scholarship is often a separate application on a separate
website, with its own deadline.

### The money across the whole set

Application fees, deposits and visa costs add up across several universities and countries. The
plan has to check that the total fits what the family can actually pay, before anyone starts
applying.

## What a finished one looks like

Only one student has completed this stage so far. Their file is private, so the description below
leaves out their name, grades and finances. The numbers and dates are real.

### The recommendation

- It ranked **16 finalists** across four countries: the United States, the United Kingdom,
  Singapore and China.
- It is about 1,800 words long.
- It opened with the balance check, and the balance check was not flattering. Of the 16, **1 was a
  Safety, 2 were a Match and 13 were a Reach**. The document said so directly, before ranking
  anything, and told the student the list "does not have a second line of defence".
- It caught the Oxford and Cambridge rule. Both were on the student's list. The document said the
  student had to pick one before the deadline, and said which one fitted their first priority
  better and why.
- It caught a problem that had nothing to do with ranking. At one university the course the student
  liked was not on the official list of accredited engineering degrees that Malaysia's engineering
  board relies on, while a near-identical course at the same university was. The recommendation
  said to apply for the second one.
- It ended with six open risks. One was an English test that had not been booked yet. Another was
  that no backup route had been written down for any of the sixteen if the exam results came in
  lower than predicted.

A list of sixteen is far more than the three to five this stage was designed around. That is
discussed under "Honest limits" below.

### The calendar

- **78 dated lines**, covering 17 finalists.
- They fall on 41 different days, from June 2026 to October 2027. That is sixteen months of dates.
- 17 of the lines are application deadlines taken from the spreadsheet. The other **61 came from
  the report researchers' notes**: test bookings, scholarship deadlines, interviews, visa dates.

That last number is the reason the calendar is worth having. If we had only listed application
deadlines, the student would have seen 17 dates and missed 61.

Here are seven of the 78 lines, for the UK only, with the wording lightly simplified:

| Date | What | Who | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| 28 Sep 2026 | Booking closes for the entrance test | Cambridge | Booking must be complete. No late entry |
| 12 Oct 2026 | Entrance test window opens (12 to 16 October) | Cambridge | Sit the maths and physics papers |
| 15 Oct 2026 | UCAS deadline for Cambridge, 6pm UK time | Cambridge | Submit the UCAS form |
| 22 Oct 2026 | Deadline for Cambridge's own extra form | Cambridge | Submit the form, a short statement, the transcript and a £60 fee |
| 7 Dec 2026 | Interviews begin (7 to 18 December) | Cambridge | Take the interview online from Malaysia |
| 13 Jan 2027 | UCAS deadline for most other courses, 6pm UK time | Heriot-Watt | The form must have arrived at UCAS |
| 1 Mar 2027 | Earliest day to apply for a UK student visa | Heriot-Watt | Apply; the required money must already have sat in the bank for 28 days |

Read down that table and three lessons appear that no single report would have taught:

1. **The first deadline is not the application.** The entrance test has to be booked seventeen
   days before the application is even due. A student watching only "15 October" would have been
   locked out on 28 September.
2. **Two universities on the same form can be three months apart.** Cambridge's line is in
   October. Heriot-Watt's is in January.
3. **Some deadlines are invisible.** The visa line says the money must already have been in the
   bank for 28 days. The real deadline for having the money ready is therefore a month before the
   date on the page.

### An invented example: what the reasoning sounds like

Mei is the invented student from our intake report. **She is not a real person.** She wants
Mechanical Engineering in the UK, her grades are predicted because her results arrive in January
2027, and she has not taken an English test.

Suppose her finalists are Oxford, Cambridge, Imperial College London and two less selective
universities. Her recommendation would have to tell her four things she may not have realised:

- **She has to choose between Oxford and Cambridge**, and do it before mid-October, months before
  her exam results exist.
- **Three of her five are long shots.** Her other two choices are carrying the whole plan, so they
  must be universities she would truly be content to attend. If they are not, the list needs
  changing now, while it still can be.
- **Everything rests on predictions.** The plan must name which choices survive if she drops a
  grade, and which university offers a foundation year as a way back in.
- **The English test is on her critical path.** It goes on the calendar with a date early enough
  to allow one retake.

None of those four points is new research. All of them come from putting the facts side by side
and reading them against the rules. That is the work this stage does.

## What this stage leaves out, on purpose

- **It does not track the applications themselves.** It records how and when to apply. It does not
  record whether the student has submitted, been offered a place or paid a deposit. A tracker like
  that is noted as a possible later addition.
- **It does not write the student's essays** or coach them for tests or interviews.
- **It does not submit anything.** The student applies. We never apply on their behalf.
- **It does not promise an outcome.** The instruction to the AI is to present a recommendation and
  the trade-offs, and not to over-promise.

The fill-in-the-form detail (exactly which boxes, which financial forms, which documents for which
system) lives in a separate **apply guide**, written once per country or region. This stage answers
"which ones, in what order, by when". The apply guide answers "now exactly what do I type".
Together the two are sold as the third tier of the service, the **Apply Pack**.

## Where things stand today

From the project's progress report and a count of the student files on 2026-10-10:

| Where the student is | Students |
|---|---|
| Finished this stage | **1** |
| All their reports are written, and this stage has not been run yet | **14** |
| Still at an earlier step (building the list, or a report in progress) | 14 |
| **Total signed up** | **29** |

So this is the least-used part of the service by a wide margin. One student in twenty-nine has
received it, the same number as when the product review was written two weeks earlier.

A closer look at the 14 who are waiting explains part of that:

- **9 of them have exactly one finalist.** With a single favourite there is nothing to rank and no
  balance to check. For these students the stage shrinks to a calendar and a how-to-apply recap.
- **5 of them have two or more finalists** (between two and six each). These are the students for
  whom the full stage is ready to run today.

This looks like a mismatch in how the service is offered. The sales poster invites a student to
"pick 1 uni to go deep on", and most students did exactly that. The decision stage was designed for
a student choosing among three to five. That reading is the author's interpretation of the counts,
not something students have told us.

## Honest limits

Listed with the most serious first.

- **Nothing inspects the recommendation automatically.** The spreadsheet and the reports each have
  an inspector program that must pass before anything reaches a student. The recommendation has
  none. Its quality rests on the AI model and on the founder reading it. A missing balance check or
  a forgotten Oxford-or-Cambridge clash would not be caught by a machine.

- **The calendar's richest ingredient is stored in a temporary place.** The researchers' dated
  notes are kept in a working folder that the project treats as disposable. For the one student
  who has a calendar, that folder has since been cleared. The calendar document itself is safe.
  But if the calendar program were run again for that student today, it would find only the 17
  spreadsheet deadlines and would replace the 78-line calendar with a 17-line one. Of the 14
  students waiting for this stage, only 3 still have those notes on file. The repair is small
  (keep the notes in the student's permanent folder) and has not been made yet.

- **American universities bring fewer dates into the calendar.** Reports on US universities are
  saved under a different file name from other reports, and the calendar program does not look for
  that name. In the one calendar we have, all eight US universities appear with their main
  deadline only. Their other dates are covered in the separate apply guide for the US.

- **The calendar only places a date on the timeline if it is written one exact way** (year, month,
  day, in numbers). Across all 42 finalist rows on file today, 33 are written that way. The other
  9 are blank, marked "not published", only approximate, or written in words, such as
  "13 Jan 2027". Those are not
  lost. They appear in the "to confirm" list. But they are not sorted into the timeline, so a real
  date written in words would sit at the bottom instead of in its proper place.

- **The same event can appear twice.** The spreadsheet and a researcher's note can both record one
  deadline. The program does not merge them. In the one real calendar, 8 of the 78 lines are
  repeats of this kind.

- **The calendar does not know what day it is.** It does not mark dates that have passed or
  highlight what is next. Five of the 78 lines were already in the past on the day it was made.
  Pointing out the next action is done by a person. It also sends no reminders, and it does not
  update itself if a university moves a date.

- **The recommendation is written as a working note, not a finished handout.** The one we have
  refers to the student by name in the third person and mentions internal file names. The
  university reports are held to a rule that they speak to the student as "you" and are delivered
  as a designed PDF. This stage has no such rule yet, and no PDF step of its own.

- **It was designed for three to five finalists and its one real run had seventeen.** A side-by-side
  table seventeen columns wide is hard to read, and a ranking of sixteen is long. The stage
  coped, but it has never been run at the size it was built for.

- **It still needs the founder to run it,** and how long it takes or what it costs has not been
  measured.

## What this means for the business

**This is the step where the product pays off for the customer.** The family's fear is spending
RM 200,000 to RM 400,000 on the wrong university, or missing a deadline. The list and the reports
reduce the first fear. This stage, with the apply guide beside it, is what addresses the second.
It is also the point where the research turns into something the student does on a specific day.

**It is probably the cheapest step to deliver.** The two stages before it are mostly research:
reading university websites, which is where the AI usage and the hours go. This stage does almost
no new research. It rearranges and reasons over facts already collected, and two of its four steps
are programs with no AI at all. The cost has not been measured, so this is a reasoned expectation and
not a figure.

**It is also the least delivered, and the third tier is being sold on one example.** The product
review already names this as one of its three most serious findings. The counts above add one
detail: five students could receive the full stage now, without any further research being done
first.

Put those three points together and the opportunity is plain. The step with the lowest cost and
the most direct link to what the customer is afraid of is the one almost nobody has been given.
