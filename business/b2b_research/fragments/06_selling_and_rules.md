# Selling to education businesses: how they buy, where they meet, and the rules

*Stream 6. Researched 2026-10-15.*

This stream looks across the four buyer types covered in streams 1–4: agents and portals, paid consultants, pre-university (pre-U) colleges, international schools and scholarship bodies, and private universities. It covers how each one buys, where they meet, which rules bind them, what a B2B (business-to-business) price could look like, and the legal duties you take on when you handle *their* students' data. Organisation profiles are in streams 1–5 and are not repeated here.

## 1. Summary in 5 lines

1. **Every buyer type buys differently.** Agents and consultants are owner-run, so one person decides. Private universities buy through a group procurement office (the department that handles purchasing). MARA buys through formal quotations open only to **Bumiputera suppliers registered with the Ministry of Finance**, a gate that a sole trader may not pass [Known, MARA notice; Guess on fit].
2. **Where they meet:** Star Education Fairs (about 70 institutions and 100 booths in KL in 2025), the British Council's agent and counsellor training hub and its forums, the AIMS (Association of International Malaysian Schools) conferences, MAPCU and NAPEI (the two private-college associations), and ICEF workshops, which in 2026 were held outside Malaysia.
3. **PDPA (Personal Data Protection Act 2010), as amended in 2024, now binds you directly as a "data processor".** You must keep the data secure (fines up to RM1 million or 3 years in prison). You must tell your client "promptly" about any breach, because their 72-hour clock to tell the regulator starts when you tell them. If you reuse their student data for your own purposes, you stop being a processor. A Data Protection Officer is only required above 20,000 people (or 10,000 for sensitive or financial data) [Known, Act A1727 and the Commissioner's guidelines].
4. **Agent codes lean towards honest lists.** The UK National Code says agents must give advice tailored to each student's "capacity to pay" and say which universities they represent and which they do not. Australia's rules make universities responsible for their agents acting "in the best interests of the student", and since January 2026 they ban commission on onshore transfers (students switching between Australian providers). Malaysia itself has no licence for agents who send students abroad, as far as I could find [Unknown].
5. **Real price points exist for nearby products:** about £25 per student profile (Morrisby, a careers-test platform for UK schools), £750 a year for an international-school licence, €24.90–19.99 per lead (one European listings platform), and ICEF's €325 a year for agency accreditation. I found **no published white-label or retainer price** in Malaysia. Pain-point evidence is strongest in job ads (fresh graduates on sales incentives doing course guidance) and weakest in forums (Reddit and Lowyat returned nothing usable).

## 3. Customers

Who pays whom. This is the conflict test from `competitors.md`, applied to the B2B buyer:

- **Agents and portals:** students are the users, universities pay [Known, competitors.md]. If an agent buys your list, the agent pays you out of commission income earned from partner universities. Your list's "any university" coverage then works against the agent's own income [Guess].
- **Consultants:** families pay them [Known, stream 2], so an honest list lines up with what their client wants.
- **International schools:** parents pay school fees, and the school buys software from that budget [Known, stream 3/5]. The school's "customer" is the parent, which lines up too.
- **Pre-U colleges:** students pay the college, but some colleges also earn agent commission (Taylor's is on Liverpool's agent list; stream 3). The incentives are mixed.
- **Scholarship bodies:** the state or a company pays. Its "customer" is its own budget and policy, and the universities a scholar may choose are fixed by an approved list (stream 3).
- **Private universities:** they pay to *win* students (stream 4). They are the most conflicted buyer of an any-university list.

**Data angle:** In every B2B deal, the business is the **data controller** (the party that decides why and how student data is used) and you would be its **data processor** (the party that handles the data only on the controller's instructions) [Known definitions, PDPA s4]. Private HEIs and private schools must already be registered with the PDP Commissioner as "Education" class data controllers. **No registration class covers education agents or consultants** [Known, pdp.gov.my class list]. So colleges, universities and international schools already have PDPA paperwork habits, and agents may not [Guess].

## 5. Pain points

These are shared across buyer types and backed by evidence. Per-type detail is in streams 1–4.

1. **List-building is done by junior, sales-incentivised staff.** One JobStreet search on 15 Oct 2026 showed "90 Study Abroad jobs in Malaysia" [Known]. The wording in those ads:
   - Uni Enrol, Junior Education Consultant (University Abroad Placement), **RM3,000–4,500**: "Guide students in choosing suitable courses, applying for scholarships, and securing admission to colleges and universities overseas and in Malaysia". The Senior role uses the same line at RM5,000–6,000 [Known].
   - EduAdvisor, Education Consultant, RM3,000–4,500: "Fresh graduates and experienced candidates welcome" [Known].
   - INTI, "Education Counselor, International Office | Fresh Graduate": "Attractive Sale Incentive Commission"; "Counseling, sales & marketing activities to promote programmes offered by the college" [Known].
   - IDP, Education Counselor (AU/NZ), KL, RM3,100–4,300: "To finalize applications, provide excellent counselling services" [Known].
   - PSB Academy, recruitment executive based in Malaysia: "meet student recruitment and revenue targets" [Known].

   *Reading:* the person choosing a student's universities is often a new graduate paid partly on enrolments. That means high turnover of course knowledge and a built-in sales tilt [Guess from the ad wording].
2. **Rules now ask for advice tailored to the student, including money.** The UK National Code (section 4.1) asks agents for information "tailored to the individual student client's circumstances, particularly in relation to language skills, capacity to pay and level of study". The evidence it suggests includes "Spot check audit documents of counselling interactions" [Known]. Proving this costs time; a saved, dated shortlist with MYR totals is one way to provide the proof [Guess].
3. **Commission scrutiny is rising in Australia.** Since January 2026 there has been a ban on commission for onshore transfers, with students accepted on or before 31 March 2026 exempt [Known, education.gov.au]. The 2025 ESOS (Education Services for Overseas Students) amendments add "collection of commissions information" and "transparency of education agent information" [Known]. Agents who rely on commission face more audit of their recommendations [Guess].
4. **PDPA compliance has become a real cost for every buyer.** Since 1 June 2025 there is a duty to report breaches to the Commissioner within 72 hours and to affected people within 7 days after that. Failing to notify carries up to RM250,000 or 2 years in prison [Known, DBN guideline]. Every new vendor that touches student data adds contract work [Guess].
5. **Counsellor capacity is thin.** Government schools run at 1 counsellor to 500 students against a target of 1 to 250, and those counsellors mostly handle mental health [Known, NST Aug 2026 / Malay Mail Jul 2026; also stream 3].
6. **Forum and review evidence: missing.** Reddit returned nothing (blocked to the scraper). The Lowyat threads I reached were about school fees, not agents. No Malaysian news story on an agent pushing partner universities turned up (searched The Star, SAYS, Malaysiakini wording) [Unknown]. This matches stream 2's gap. **Interviews are the only route.**

## 6. How they shortlist today

From the rules angle (who builds the list is in streams 1–4):

- **UK-facing agents** work to the National Code. They should "be transparent about which organisations the company formally represent (under contract) and those that they do not" (1.4), "act in the best interests of a qualified student" (4.3), and say which services are "educational institution pays" (commission) versus "student pays" (2.2). They need not disclose the commission amount, which is "commercially sensitive" (footnote 1) [Known]. The Code "is not in of itself a legal document". UK universities write it into agent contracts [Known]. The version I opened is the 2021 edition; a July 2025 edition exists [snippet only].
- **Australia-facing agents** (including those in Malaysia) are bound through their university's contract. National Code Standard 4.1 requires "a written agreement with each education agent" and the agent's details in PRISMS (the Australian government's student-enrolment database). Standard 4.3 requires agents to "act honestly and in good faith, and in the best interests of the student" and to know the "Australian International Education and Training Agent Code of Ethics". TEQSA (Australia's higher-education regulator) "does not regulate education agents"; the universities must police them [Known, TEQSA page].
- **Training and credentials:** British Council certification (free, online, public database) [Known]; PIER's QEAC (Qualified Education Agent Counsellor, for Australia, exam €295 via ICEF) [snippet]; ICEF accreditation (€325 a year) [Known].
- **Malaysia:** I found **no licence or code for agents sending students abroad**. Universities set their own agent rules: for example, UTM asks for one year of recruiting experience and two Malaysian referees (that is for inbound agents) [snippet only]. [Unknown: searched MOHE/KPT and EMGS agent rules.]
- **Scholarship bodies** shortlist inside approved-university lists (stream 3). That is policy, not a code.

## 7. Fit with an honest, family-first list

**Where the rules help you** [Guess unless tagged]:
- The UK Code's "capacity to pay" and "best interests" lines, and Australia's Standard 4.3, are exactly what a total-cost MYR list with Reach/Match/Safety evidences. You could pitch it as **compliance evidence** ("a dated record that you tailored advice to this family's budget") rather than "honest advice". That avoids implying the agent is dishonest.
- Agents already pay small yearly sums for credibility (ICEF €325 [Known]), so a small per-year fee is not foreign to them.

**Where the rules cost you (PDPA as a data processor)** [Known from Act A1727, the Act 709 text and the Commissioner's guidelines, unless tagged]:

| Duty | What it means for you | In force |
|---|---|---|
| **Security Principle now binds processors directly** (s5(1A), s9) | You must take "practical steps" to protect student data. Breach: fine up to **RM1,000,000** or up to **3 years** in prison, or both (raised from RM300,000 / 2 years) | 1 Apr 2025 |
| **Breach notification** (s12B) | Does "not directly apply to data processor", but the controller "is required to contractually impose" that you "promptly notify" it and help it. Its 72-hour clock starts "once the data processor notifies" it | 1 Jun 2025 |
| **DPO** (Data Protection Officer) (s12A) | Processors must appoint one too, but only above **20,000 data subjects**, **10,000 for "sensitive personal data including financial information"**, or for "regular and systematic monitoring". The DPO must be resident in Malaysia (180+ days a year) or easily contactable, and proficient in Bahasa Melayu and English. The role can be outsourced | 1 Jun 2025 |
| **Cross-border transfer** (s129) | The ministerial whitelist (an approved list of countries) is gone. The controller may transfer where the other country's law is substantially similar or protection is adequate, or under listed exceptions [Known, Rajah & Tann; partly A1727]. If your pipeline runs on overseas cloud, AI or search services, your *client* has to be satisfied with that [Guess] | 1 Apr 2025 |
| **Processor status itself** (s4) | A processor processes "solely on behalf of" the controller and "does not process the personal data for any of his own purposes". Reusing a client's student data to improve your own product or database would make you a controller, with the full set of duties [Known definition; Guess on application] | Since 2013 |
| **Minors** (s4) | For anyone under 18, the "relevant person" is the parent or guardian. Many pre-U students are 17 [Guess on ages], so consent runs through parents | Since 2013 |

Your volumes are far below the DPO thresholds [Guess]. But family-budget fields could count as "financial information" [Guess], so keep that field optional or coarse. Expect buyers who are registered data controllers (colleges, universities, international schools) to send you a data processing agreement (a contract setting out these duties) [Guess].

**Conflict summary:** consultants and international schools have no money-flow conflict. Agents and pre-U colleges have a mixed one; the compliance framing is the softest way in. Private universities have a direct conflict (stream 4). Scholarship bodies have no conflict, but government procurement favours MOF-registered Bumiputera firms, which may be a practical barrier [Guess].

## 9. Questions to ask them

Ask about past behaviour and current spend, never "would you buy?"

1. The last time you started paying a new outside service, who first suggested it, who signed, and how long from first meeting to payment?
2. Did that vendor have to go through a procurement or vendor-registration step? What forms, and how long did it take?
3. Since PDPA's 2025 changes, has any vendor had to sign a data processing agreement with you? What did you insist on (breach-notice hours, storage location, deletion)?
4. Who is your DPO, or who handles PDPA? When did they last review a vendor?
5. (Agents) When a partner university last audited you against the UK National Code or Australia's Standard 4, what did they ask to see? Did you have it ready?
6. (Agents) What did you pay last year for ICEF, British Council or PIER credentials, fairs and agent workshops? Which paid off?
7. (Agents and portals) How long has your newest consultant been with you? How did they learn which universities suit a student, and how long did that take?
8. (Schools) What do you pay per year for Cialfo, Unifrog, BridgeU or similar? Is it per student or flat? When does the budget get set?
9. (All) The last time a family asked what the whole degree would cost in ringgit, what did you give them, and how long did it take to put together?
10. (Fair exhibitors) What did your last Star Education Fair booth cost, and how many enrolments did you trace back to it?
11. (Scholarship bodies) Have you ever paid anyone outside to help place scholars? How was that bought?
