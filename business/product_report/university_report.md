# The University Report — what it is and how it gets made

*Written 2026-10-10 for a reader with no technical background. Student and feedback numbers come from
the product review dated 2026-09-25.*

---

## The short version

A Malaysian family choosing an overseas university is making a decision that costs roughly
RM 200,000 to RM 400,000 (ringgit, Malaysia's currency) and takes three to four years of a young
person's life. Our service helps a student make that decision in four steps. This document explains
the third step, the **University Report**.

A University Report is a fifteen-section document, delivered as a PDF, about **one** university that
**one** student is seriously considering. It is written to that student personally, using their
grades, their budget and the things they said matter most to them. It answers three questions:

1. **Can I get in?**
2. **Will I belong there and do well?**
3. **What will applying demand of me?**

A student gets one report for each of their favourite universities, usually three to five of them.

It is the most research-heavy thing we make. That is why we only make it for the few universities
that have already survived the earlier, cheaper steps.

---

## Where it sits in the whole service

| Step | Name | What the student ends up with |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Intake** | A short form that records who they are: grades, budget, countries, subject, and what they care about most. |
| 2 | **The List** | A spreadsheet of candidate universities, one row each, with the whole degree costed in ringgit. |
| 3 | **The University Report** | A deep document on each of their 3 to 5 favourites. *This is the subject of this document.* |
| 4 | **The Decision** | A ranked recommendation and one calendar holding every deadline. |

A small naming note, in case you see it elsewhere: inside our own files this step is labelled
"Stage 4". The numbering is left over from an earlier design where intake was two separate steps.
It is the third thing a student experiences.

The idea that holds the four steps together is this: **the deeper the research, the fewer
universities it is spent on.** Looking up thirty universities in a little detail is cheap. Looking up
one university in great detail is expensive. So we go wide and shallow first, let the student cut the
list down, and only then go deep.

---

## Why the spreadsheet is not enough

The List is built to be skimmed. Every cell holds one short line, on purpose, so a student can run
their eye down thirty universities and cross most of them off.

That makes it good at **comparing** and poor at **deciding**. A row can tell you a degree costs
RM 380,000 and needs three A grades. It has no room to tell you:

- what kind of person tends to be happy at that university, and whether you are that kind of person;
- what the students who were actually admitted had besides their grades;
- whether you can find halal food (food prepared according to Islamic dietary rules) near your room;
- that one particular rule could cost you your funding if you miss it.

Those things need paragraphs, tables and warnings. The University Report is where they live.

---

## What is inside a report

Every report has the same fifteen sections in the same order, whichever country the university is in.
Keeping the order fixed means a student can put two reports side by side and find the same
information in the same place.

| # | Section | What it tells the student | The question it serves |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Snapshot | A one-table summary: university, course, city, rankings, how you apply, your chance of getting in, and your own priorities listed in order. | Orientation |
| 2 | Identity and what it's known for | What the university is trying to be, and what it is famous for. | Who is this place? |
| 3 | Who it's for | The kind of student the place attracts and shapes, and an honest read on whether you fit. | Who is this place? |
| 4 | Getting in | The entry requirements, how your grades compare, what admitted students really looked like, and what the application makes you submit. | Can I get in? |
| 5 | What you'll actually study | The subjects, how long it takes, and how hard it is to change course once you start. | What would I study? |
| 6 | Signature academic experiences | Named opportunities such as undergraduate research, work placements and study abroad. | What would I study? |
| 7 | Culture and vibe | Whether students compete or cooperate, how heavy the workload is, how teaching is done. | Will I belong? |
| 8 | Student life, housing and food | Where you live, what you eat, clubs, the first-year experience, and links to real students describing it. | Will I belong? |
| 9 | The city, the area and belonging | Safety, transport, things to do, the Malaysian community, prayer facilities. | Will I belong? |
| 10 | Costs (full) | Tuition, living costs, fees and deposit, added into one total for the whole degree in ringgit. | Can I afford it? |
| 11 | Scholarships and financial aid | Funding from the university, from governments and from Malaysian sponsors, with who qualifies for each. | Can I afford it? |
| 12 | Outcomes, network and practising in Malaysia | Graduate jobs and salaries, whether the degree is accepted back home, and whether you may stay and work after graduating. | What is the payoff? |
| 13 | Unique facts and quirks | Traditions and notable graduates in your field. The detail that makes the place feel real. | Colour |
| 14 | Why here / why hesitate | Two to four reasons to choose this university **and** the reasons to hesitate, tied to your priorities. | Should I? |
| 15 | Sources | Every source used, labelled as official or not, with the admission year it applies to. | Can I trust this? |

Four things about how a report is written matter as much as what it covers:

- **It speaks to the student as "you".** It is their guide. It is not a case file about them.
- **It says why you might not want to go.** Section 14 must include reasons to hesitate. A document
  that praises every university is one nobody believes.
- **A rule that could wreck the plan gets a red warning box.** For example, at some American
  universities an overseas student who does not ask for financial aid when they first apply is barred
  from asking for it later. That sentence must never be buried in the middle of a paragraph.
- **Abbreviations are explained automatically.** The PDF adds a "Key terms" box listing every
  abbreviation that report uses, and the first time each one appears the student can tap it to jump
  to its meaning.

## How a report gets made

There are seven steps. The first two happen in conversation with the student. The rest are research
and checking.

### Step 1: The student chooses

We show the student a side-by-side comparison of the universities on their list: cost, chance of
getting in, scholarships, and fit. Then we wait. **The student picks the three to five they want to
go deep on. The system is not allowed to pick for them.** A university someone else chose for you is
one you will not trust the report on.

### Step 2: A cheap check before the expensive work

Before any deep research starts, each pick gets a quick inspection. A report is costly to make, and
making one for a university whose deadline passed last week is money thrown away. We check:

- **Is the deadline still open, and is the course still offered for that start date?** This is the
  fact that goes out of date fastest.
- **Were this university's facts checked on an official source?** An official source is the
  university's own website, the national application service for that country, the government's visa
  office, or the Malaysian body that recognises degrees. If a pick was never checked that way, it
  gets the full check now: fees, living costs, entry and English requirements, deadline. **A
  university cannot become a finalist on unchecked facts.**
- **Will the degree be recognised in Malaysia?** For professions the law controls (medicine,
  engineering, law, accounting), a degree that Malaysia does not recognise means the student cannot
  practise at home. We treat that as a pass-or-fail test.
- **What does the application actually demand?** Some systems that look like "grades only" are not.
  The National University of Singapore, for instance, asks every applicant to list their activities
  and answer short written questions. A student who has nothing to put there has a real problem, and
  it needs to show up in their chance of getting in.

Where the official page disagrees with what is on our list, the official figure wins and we record
the difference.

We also look at the picks as a set. Every university carries one of three labels: **Reach** (getting
in is a stretch), **Match** (a realistic chance) or **Safety** (very likely). Five Reaches is five
ways to end up with nowhere to go, so the set must include at least one Safety. If the inspection
kills a pick, it is marked rejected with a one-line reason and the student chooses a replacement
**before** any research money is spent on it.

### Step 3: The research

Each surviving university is handed to its own AI researcher. This is a separate working session of
our most capable AI model, given one university and one student's details and nothing else. Three to
five of them work at the same time, so a student's whole set of reports is produced together rather
than one after another.

The researcher gathers two different kinds of information and treats them very differently.

**Hard facts** are things with one correct answer: fees, entry requirements, deadlines, visa rules,
whether the degree is recognised. These may only come from official sources. Comparison websites
(sites that collect university information from elsewhere, which we call aggregators) are often out
of date, so they are never accepted as proof of a hard fact.

**Texture** is what it is like to be there: who gets in, how intense the work is, what students
complain about, what the city feels like. Official pages do not say these things honestly. Students
do, on forums and in videos. For texture, the researcher records the link and a short quote so the
student can go and read the original.

Two rules apply to every researcher:

- **Never invent a fact.** If something cannot be found after a real search, the report says
  "Not found" and gives the reason. A visible gap is honest. A confident guess is dangerous.
- **Try the free route first.** Ordinary web search is the default. A paid page-reading service is
  used only when the free route fails to return the fact.

### Step 4: Assembly, with a hard stop

The researcher's findings go into a program that arranges them into the fifteen sections. This
program is strict. **If any section is empty, or if there are no sources, it refuses to produce the
report at all.** A half-researched report cannot slip through looking finished.

### Step 5: An automatic inspection

A second program reads the finished report and looks for the faults that make a report weak or hard
to read:

- **Structure.** All fifteen sections are present and in the right order.
- **Summary.** Every line of the Snapshot is filled in.
- **Gaps.** More than eight "Not found" entries means the report reads as half-researched. A
  "Not found" with no reason is also flagged, because it looks the same as not having looked.
- **Sources.** There are at least ten, each with a link, a label saying whether it is official, and
  the admission year. At least half must be official.
- **Voice.** It is written to "you". The student's name does not appear, and neither do "he" or "she".
- **Layout.** Costs, scholarships, the getting-in statistics and section 14 are tables, not
  paragraphs. Warnings sit inside warning boxes.

These thresholds were set by measuring twenty real reports we had already produced, on 8 August 2026.
The researcher runs this inspection on its own work and fixes what it finds. We then run it again
across the whole batch.

### Step 6: The PDF

The checked report is turned into a PDF with page numbers, the red warning boxes and the "Key terms"
box. This happens automatically as part of the same job, so the student does not have to ask for it.

### Step 7: Updating the list

Once every report in the batch is back, a single program updates the student's spreadsheet in one
go. Each reported university is marked **Finalist**. Where the deep research showed that a one-line
summary on the list was wrong, that line is corrected.

This step is strict in three ways:

- **It will not bring back a university the student has rejected.** If the student dropped a
  university while its report was being written, the program refuses to mark it Finalist and says so.
- **It cannot change the scores or the Reach/Match/Safety label.** Those are calculated by a separate
  program from the student's grades and priorities. Nobody types them in by hand.
- **One faulty update does not block the others.** If one university's name was misspelled, the other
  four still go through, and the faulty one is reported by name.

After that, a final check runs across the whole spreadsheet. It has to pass before anything is sent
to the student.

Why update the list once at the end, instead of letting each researcher update it as they finish?
Because five researchers editing one file at the same moment can overwrite each other's work without
anyone noticing. Having each researcher leave a small "my report is done" note, and one program read
all the notes together, is what makes it safe for them to work at the same time.

## Two variations

**American universities get a whole-university report.** In most countries you apply to a specific
course. In the United States you apply to the university and usually choose your main subject at the
end of your second year. Asking "should I do this course here?" is the wrong question there, so the
report asks "should I go to this university?" instead. The fifteen sections are the same.

**The quick report.** Some students already know which university they want and only need the facts.
For them we can produce the same fifteen-section report without the intake form or the list. It
leaves out the personal parts, such as "your chance of getting in", because there is no student
profile to measure against.

---

## What the report leaves out, on purpose

The step-by-step paperwork of applying (visa forms, document checklists, the calendar of dates) is
not in the University Report. Those facts are the same for every university in a region, so we were
rewriting them once per university for no benefit. They now live in a separate apply guide, written
once per region.

## What this means for the business

The service is sold as three tiers: the List, the University Reports, and the Apply Pack. The report
is the middle tier, and it is the one students do not know to ask for. Most arrive thinking they are
getting a spreadsheet.

As of 25 September 2026:

- **15 of our 29 students** had received at least one University Report.
- Six students had rated their reports, averaging **4.2 out of 5** on time saved.

The report is also where our promise about checked facts is enforced. A university cannot be marked
Finalist until its hard facts have been confirmed on an official page.

---

## Honest limits

- **The automatic inspection judges form, not thinking.** It can tell that a costs section is a table
  with enough official sources behind it. It cannot tell whether the advice is wise. That still
  depends on the quality of the AI model and on a person reading the result.
- **Texture depends on what students have posted publicly.** A large, famous university has thousands
  of student accounts online. A small one may have very few, and its report will carry more
  "Not found" entries.
- **Ringgit figures are approximate.** Exchange rates move and fees change every year. Each source is
  stamped with its admission year so a reader can see how fresh it is.
- **The feedback sample is small.** Six ratings is encouraging, not conclusive. Every section was
  called "confusing or unnecessary" by at least one student. Section 14 split opinion most: two
  students named it the most interesting part and two named it confusing.
- **Every report still needs the founder to run it.** We have not yet published how long a student
  should expect to wait.

## Word list

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Ringgit (RM, MYR)** | Malaysia's currency. All costs are converted into it so a student can compare countries. |
| **The List** | The spreadsheet of candidate universities, one row per university and course. |
| **Finalist** | A university the student picked that has passed its checks and has a finished report. |
| **Reach / Match / Safety** | How likely the student is to be admitted: a stretch, a realistic chance, or very likely. |
| **Hard fact** | Something with one correct answer, such as a fee or a deadline. |
| **Texture** | What a place is like to live and study in, as described by the people there. |
| **Official source** | The university's own site, the national application service, the government visa office, or the Malaysian recognition body. |
| **Aggregator** | A website that collects university information from other places. Useful for finding options, not trusted for facts. |
| **Recognition** | Whether Malaysia accepts a foreign degree. The Malaysian Qualifications Agency (MQA) and the professional body for each field decide this. |
| **Regulated profession** | A job the law says you need an approved qualification for, such as doctor, engineer, lawyer or accountant. |
| **Financial aid** | Money a university gives a student to reduce what the family pays. |
| **Malaysian sponsors** | Government bodies and foundations in Malaysia that fund students, such as JPA, MARA and the Yayasan foundations. |
